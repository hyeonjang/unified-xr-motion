\begin{abstract}
XR avatar-motion pipelines often entangle device-specific tracking APIs, skeletal layouts, validity semantics, motion processing, retargeting, and deployment decisions. This entanglement makes it difficult to reuse a pipeline across headsets, hand trackers, vision-based sources, and client-server execution contexts, even when each individual SDK exposes useful tracking data. We present UnifiedXRMotion, a Unity-based prototype that separates these concerns through a schema-based, validity-aware motion-data contract, a tracking-adapter boundary, and a local/remote node-placement abstraction. The contract represents skeletal motion as addressable motion slots defined by an application schema rather than by a fixed avatar rig. Each slot may carry optional pose components guarded by binary validity flags, allowing partial trackers to publish only the components they observe. In our prototype, the contract is instantiated with a 77-slot full-body schema, but the number of slots is an implementation example rather than the conceptual contribution. We describe how SDK-specific adapters publish into the contract, how event-driven motion nodes consume it, and how a network wrapper places selected nodes across client and server contexts. We do not claim new tracking, IK, retargeting, or input-to-photon latency measurement. Instead, we make a narrower systems contribution: a concrete, inspectable runtime contract for composing heterogeneous XR avatar-motion pipelines, supported by a within-subjects authoring case study and application-level node-placement latency traces.
\keywords{Extended reality \and Avatar motion \and Tracking middleware \and Motion-data contract \and Runtime systems \and Local/remote execution}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
XR avatars depend on motion pipelines that span tracking acquisition, skeletal representation, motion processing, retargeting, and rendering. In practice these stages are frequently tied to one SDK or one avatar asset. A hand tracker may expose a vendor-specific joint order; a body tracker may report only a subset of joints; a vision model may provide positions but no reliable rotations; a retargeter may assume a particular rig hierarchy; and an offloaded node may require a serialized representation that differs from the in-process one. The result is not merely inconvenient engineering. It weakens reproducibility and makes it hard to compare pipelines, replace trackers, or move computation between client and server without rewriting glue code.

Existing XR APIs and tracking middleware address important parts of this problem. OpenXR standardizes access to XR devices and extensions such as hand tracking~\cite{khronos_openxr_1_0,openxr_ext_hand}. Earlier VR middleware, including VRPN~\cite{taylor2001vrpn}, OpenTracker~\cite{reitmayr2001openarchitecture}, and Ubiquitous Tracking~\cite{newman2004ubiquitous}, showed that device abstraction, dataflow, and distributed tracking are valuable architectural tools. These systems are direct prior art. UnifiedXRMotion does not claim novelty in generic middleware or graph execution. Its contribution is at the avatar-motion-pipeline level: a concrete runtime representation for partial skeletal motion, an adapter boundary for publishing device-specific observations into that representation, and a node-placement abstraction for local and remote execution contexts.

This paper is deliberately scoped. UnifiedXRMotion is not a new tracking algorithm, not a new inverse-kinematics solver, not a new avatar-retargeting method, and not a proof of universal device compatibility. It also is not a directed-acyclic-graph execution engine: the current runtime uses trigger-based scheduling and sender/receiver event propagation. The central design choice is to separate \emph{slot identity} from \emph{tracking availability}. A skeleton schema defines addressable motion slots and parent relations; a tracker publishes the subset it observes; each pose component is guarded by a validity flag; and downstream nodes consume only the components they require. In our Unity prototype, this contract is instantiated with a 77-slot full-body schema covering torso, limbs, head, and articulated hands. This number is not part of the contribution. It is one prototype schema instantiation. The same contract can be instantiated with smaller or larger schemas when adapters and consumers agree on the schema.

We make three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A schema-based, validity-aware motion-data contract} for XR skeletal motion, including addressable motion slots, parent relations, region views, optional pose components, binary validity flags, in-process buffer views, and validity-aware serialization.
    \item \textbf{An adapter boundary for heterogeneous tracking sources} that maps SDK-specific outputs into the shared contract. The prototype includes adapters for selected XR and vision-based sources, but we distinguish the reusable boundary from vendor-specific wrapper code.
    \item \textbf{A local/remote node-placement abstraction} that allows a motion-processing node to be wrapped for client-local, client-to-server, server-local, or server-to-client placement. We use application-level latency traces as a placement case study, not as a vendor-baseline performance claim.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Tracking middleware and runtime abstractions.}
Device-independent VR tracking is not new. VRPN provides a device-independent and network-transparent interface for VR peripherals~\cite{taylor2001vrpn}. OpenTracker introduced a modular software architecture for VR interaction and tracking configurations~\cite{reitmayr2001openarchitecture}. Ubiquitous Tracking modeled distributed spatial relationships for augmented reality as a graph of tracking transformations~\cite{newman2004ubiquitous}. UnifiedXRMotion inherits the lesson that modularity and abstraction matter, but it targets a more specific layer: runtime skeletal motion contracts for avatar pipelines, where missing joints, partial component validity, region-specific views, retargeting consumers, and optional node placement must be handled explicitly.

\paragraph{XR APIs, avatar motion, and offloading.}
OpenXR standardizes device access and extensions such as hand tracking~\cite{khronos_openxr_1_0,openxr_ext_hand}, while platform SDKs expose additional body, hand, controller, camera, or device-specific streams. These APIs tell an application how to access a source, but they do not prescribe how to represent partial skeletal motion across heterogeneous sources, mark unavailable components, or expose the same representation to filters, learned regressors, IK nodes, retargeters, and network serializers.
Sparse avatar-motion reconstruction has advanced rapidly~\cite{jiang2022avatarposer,dai2024hmd,xu2024mobileposer,yang2024divatrack,starke2024categorical}. Avatar representation and retargeting affect embodiment and agency~\cite{yun2023animationfidelity,cheymol2023beyond,weidner2023avatarsagents}, with AvatarGo focusing on plug-and-play self-avatar setup~\cite{ponton2022avatargo}. UnifiedXRMotion does not compete with these algorithms. It provides infrastructure around them: a contract through which estimators, IK solvers, and retargeters can be represented as consumers and producers in the same runtime. For offloading, recent work such as XRgo investigates rendering offload for low-power XR devices~\cite{gao2025xrgo}. UnifiedXRMotion's local/remote placement abstraction is narrower: it makes node placement explicit and exposes application-level latency traces for motion-buffer transmission.

\section{Schema-Based Motion-Data Contract}\label{sec:contract}
UnifiedXRMotion represents skeletal motion through a \emph{schema} rather than through a hard-coded claim about the human body or avatar rig. A schema defines addressable motion slots, parent relations, and optional region views. A slot is an indexable location in the motion representation, such as a head, wrist, fingertip, tracker, prop, facial control, or application-specific joint. Each slot may carry pose components such as position, rotation, velocity, and angular velocity. Each component is guarded by a binary validity flag, so a tracker can publish only the data it observes and downstream nodes can avoid reading absent components.

Partial tracking is the normal case in XR. A headset and controllers provide only a few rigid poses; a hand tracker may provide fingers but no lower body; a vision model may provide upper-body positions with unreliable rotations; and a full-body SDK may infer unobserved joints internally. UnifiedXRMotion therefore treats absence as first-class data rather than as an error. A downstream node that needs rotations can reject or ignore slots whose rotation flag is absent; a filter that only needs positions can still operate when rotations are missing.


\section{Adapter Boundary}\label{sec:adapters}
UnifiedXRMotion uses SDK-specific adapters to map native tracking outputs into the motion-data contract. The central boundary is intentionally small: an adapter initializes the native source, polls or receives tracking data, exposes the slots it can publish, and returns pose records with validity flags. The adapter code is not the research novelty; much of it is necessarily vendor-specific wrapper code. The contribution is that all adapters target the same schema and validity semantics.

\section{Runtime and Local/Remote Node Placement}\label{sec:runtime}
The runtime is an event-driven motion-node pipeline. A trigger list identifies root nodes that poll or receive new motion data. When a root node updates, it invokes a sender event; downstream receivers subscribe to that event, read the upstream motion contract through buffer views, write their own output records, and publish another update. This design is intentionally simple and Unity-friendly. It should not be described as topological graph scheduling. There is no general cycle detector or graph optimizer in the current implementation.

\section{Implementation}\label{sec:implementation}
The prototype is implemented as a Unity package. Its core implementation separates four concerns. First, motion slots are stored in preallocated buffers. Region views expose subsets of the schema, allowing a hand-only node, upper-body node, or full-body node to operate on the subset it needs. In-process view access avoids copying the entire pose record for each node. Network transmission necessarily serializes records and is not zero-copy. Second, SDK-specific adapters publish native tracking data into the shared contract. Some adapters are direct wrappers over vendor SDKs; others support synthetic transforms, replay, or vision-based sources. Third, the prototype includes IK, retargeting, codebook, filtering, record, and replay nodes. These nodes demonstrate that the contract can serve multiple consumers, but they are not claimed as new algorithms. Textbook solvers such as FABRIK~\cite{aristidou2011fabrik} and codebook-style control inspired by prior work~\cite{starke2024categorical} should be cited as algorithmic background rather than framed as the paper's novelty. Fourth, the implementation includes CSV and histogram export utilities for timing traces, plus record/replay utilities that can support reproducible evaluation. A stronger benchmark paper would require a released replay dataset, analysis scripts, and round-trip validation tests.

\section{Evaluation and Case Study Evidence}\label{sec:evaluation}
The evidence in this medium paper is deliberately scoped. We do not claim a complete performance evaluation, a vendor-native runtime baseline, an avatar-quality study, or universal device coverage. Instead, we report three forms of evidence that match the paper's narrowed systems contribution: contract and adapter coverage, a within-subjects setup-burden case study, and application-level traces illustrating local/remote node placement.

\subsection{Contract and Adapter Coverage}
Tables~\ref{tab:contract}, \ref{tab:adapters}, and~\ref{tab:orig-wrapper} summarize the implementation evidence for the motion contract, adapter boundary, and original-vs-wrapper separation. The important result is not the number of slots or adapters; it is the separation of concerns. Device-specific logic is localized in adapters, while downstream nodes operate on the shared contract. This supports the claim that UnifiedXRMotion implements a concrete schema-based contract and demonstrates it with selected sources. It does not prove generality across all XR devices.

\subsection{Authoring Case Study: Setup Burden}\label{sec:userstudy}
We conducted a within-subjects Unity setup task comparing a UnifiedXRMotion workflow against a vendor SDK workflow. Participants configured the same scene-level goal in both conditions: making custom OpenXR hand prefabs follow simulated hand motion and making the same Y Bot avatar follow simulated full-body motion. Both conditions used the Meta XR Simulator for verification, including random full-body motion and hand motion or pinch gestures. The study used Unity 6000.0.33f1 and Meta Core, Movement SDK, and XR Simulator version 78.0.0.

To avoid conflating pipeline assembly with mesh-specific hand-axis calibration, custom hand retargeting and axis alignment were completed before each session. This made the comparison conservative with respect to UnifiedXRMotion's automatic hand-retargeting support, but it also means the study does not measure the full cost of integrating a previously unseen hand mesh. Participants were instructed to prioritize correctness and completion rather than speed. We therefore interpret completion time as a coarse measure of procedural setup burden, not as a pure speed-optimization measure.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{Why a motion contract paper is defensible.} The previous framing as a graph framework invites the wrong comparison and overstates the implementation. The defensible research object is the contract. In avatar-motion pipelines, the hard engineering problem is not only acquiring tracking data; it is keeping track of what a source actually observed, representing that partial data consistently, and making downstream nodes robust to missing components. UnifiedXRMotion addresses that problem through schema-defined slots and component-level validity.

\paragraph{What the abstraction enables.} The contract lets adapters, filters, retargeters, and network serializers share a common representation. It also makes missing data explicit. This is valuable for XR because different trackers expose different body regions and component reliability. A hand source, a sparse controller source, a replay stream, and a vision source can all publish partial motion into the same runtime representation, provided that they agree on the schema.

\paragraph{Where the abstraction is still weak.} The contract is not yet a stable standard. It lacks mandatory timestamps, coordinate-frame metadata, continuous confidence, and schema versioning. These omissions matter. Without timestamps, latency and synchronization claims are limited. Without coordinate-frame metadata, frame conversions remain adapter-specific and easy to misconfigure. Without continuous confidence, vision trackers lose information when confidence is collapsed into binary validity. Without schema versioning, external tools cannot safely exchange saved motion streams across versions.

\paragraph{Local/remote placement remains structural.} The network wrapper is a useful systems abstraction, but structural reuse is not the same as equivalence. A wrapped node may receive data at different times, serialize different subsets, or run under different scheduling conditions. A future version should run the same recorded motion stream through local and remote placements and report per-slot output differences.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
This work has significant limitations. First, it does not measure input-to-photon or motion-to-photon latency. The reported traces exclude sensor sampling, SDK internal processing, compositor scheduling, rendering, display scan-out, and photon emission. Second, the prototype does not prove universal device support. It implements selected adapters, but each new tracker still requires a schema mapping, coordinate conversion, and validation. Third, the current 77-slot schema is one prototype instantiation; arbitrary avatar rigs require schema agreement and retargeting between the runtime representation and the avatar-specific skeleton. Fourth, UnifiedXRMotion does not introduce new tracking, IK, or retargeting algorithms. The included solvers and retargeters are representative consumers of the contract.

The authoring case study also has limits. Hand-axis alignment and mesh-specific retargeting were pre-completed before sessions, so the study isolates pipeline assembly rather than full new-avatar integration. Participants were instructed to prioritize correctness and task completion, so completion time should be interpreted as procedural setup burden, not pure speed. The study does not measure adapter-authoring effort or long-term maintainability. Finally, this medium paper does not include a bytes-per-frame wire-format benchmark, a serialization round-trip test, a deterministic local/remote equivalence check, or a vendor-native runtime latency baseline. These are appropriate future validations for a longer systems paper, but they are not claimed here.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
UnifiedXRMotion reframes heterogeneous XR avatar-motion integration as a contract problem. Rather than proposing a universal skeleton, a new tracker, or a DAG execution engine, it defines a schema-based validity-aware representation in which trackers publish partial motion into addressable slots and downstream nodes consume only valid components. The prototype demonstrates this contract through selected SDK adapters, event-driven motion nodes, representative retargeting and IK consumers, and a local/remote node-placement wrapper. The current evidence is not sufficient for broad latency or avatar-quality claims, but it supports a narrower EuroXR contribution: a concrete runtime contract and abstraction boundary for composing heterogeneous XR avatar-motion pipelines, with supporting evidence from a setup-burden case study and local/remote placement traces.
